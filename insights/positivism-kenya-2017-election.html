The Application of Positivism in Kenya: An Analysis of The Supreme Court Decision in Odinga & another v Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission & 2 others (2017) eKLR

By Elijah Chacha

View on SSRN →

Abstract

Positivism is not an abstract concept that is worth academic debates alone. No wonder, during its development, legal scholars and thinkers spent much of their time developing this concept through arguments and counterarguments. The Hart-Fuller debate is living testimony to this assertion. Gone are the days when such debates were appreciated only in the abstract; this is not to say that they are not in this era. However, they have decreased significantly. It is against this backdrop that this paper demonstrates how the Supreme Court of Kenya applied legal positivism to nullify the Kenyan presidential election in 2017. It argues that legal schools of thought and their applications are not overtaken by events because they cannot be. The paper argues that in the wake of people-centred constitutions such as the Constitution of Kenya 2010, where power is directly derived from the people, legal positivism finds a fertile platform for its application. Re Raila Odinga was chosen to illustrate this proposition.

Application of Positivism in the Kenyan Supreme Court

The Supreme Court of Kenya in nullifying the 2017 presidential elections — the concurring judges adopted the legal positivism school of thought. Legal positivism means that a law is created by acts of human beings which take place in time and space, in contradistinction to natural law, which is supposed to originate in another way. The Court ruled: "The moment we ignore our Constitution the Kenyans fought for decades, we lose it." The reasoning of the Court was that, since it is the people who make the laws and donate the power to the courts, it is imperative that the election that never reflected the will of the people be nullified. This is John Austin's paradigm that "law cannot be defined by the inclusion of any ideal of justice and that law must be determined by reference to its source, i.e., the sovereign." The majority held that the sovereign is the people. It thus held:

"What of the argument that this Court should not subvert the will of the people? This Court is one of those to whom that sovereign power has been delegated under Article 1(3)(c) of the same Constitution. All its powers including that of invalidating a presidential election is not self-given nor forcefully taken, but is donated by the people of Kenya. To dishonestly exercise that delegated power and to close our eyes to constitutional violations would be a dereliction of duty and we refuse to accept the invitation to do so however popular the invitation may seem."

From the above juxtaposition, the Court relied heavily on the positivist principle that a law is a legal rule even if it is not just. It agreed with the petitioners that 'where an election is not conducted in accordance with the Constitution and other written laws, then that election must be invalidated notwithstanding the fact that the results were not affected.' The results not being affected is the just outcome of an election, however as the positivist the Court was, it strictly applied the law as it is. Furthermore, the majority decision was premised on violation of the aspirations and wishes of the Grundnorm, which is an inbuilt power behind the Constitution — the people. As Kendi Mutungi and Murimi Karani argued, "The Court's decision was majorly qualitative premised on results transmission and complementary mechanisms which failed to comply with the constitutional principles of 'integrity, transparency, accuracy, accountability, impartiality, simplicity, verifiability, security and efficiency' (Articles 10, 81(e) and 86 of the Constitution)."

As positivists define law not by reference to its content but according to the formal criteria which differentiate legal rules from other rules like morals, the majority's decision adopted a similar view when they ruled that "an election is a process and not an event such that it did not matter who won if the process was tainted." The process is as provided by the Constitution and the electoral laws. This is the backdrop against which the Court nullified the election as argued by Victoria Miyandazi: "the Independent Electoral and Boundaries Commission (IEBC) failed to conduct the Presidential Election in a manner consistent with the dictates of the Constitution and the Elections Act."

The Court Adopts Kelsen's Pure Theory of Law

The Court also adopted Hans Kelsen's idea that "in truth all law is judge-made law, inasmuch as — quite aside even from judicial precedents — it is the courts that give legislative enactments their true meaning and delineation." The court gave Section 83 of the Elections Act its true meaning and delineation by interpreting it. It ruled:

"In our respectful view, the two limbs of Section 83 of the Elections Act should be applied disjunctively. In the circumstances, a petitioner who is able to satisfactorily prove either of the two limbs of the Section can void an election."

Therefore, the judges made the law by applying it. Furthermore, the majority's decision was a pure theory of law, applying the law as it is. As the critics of positivism would put it — a logical abstraction devoid of real life. The Court, as if being aware of its positivist approach, addressed this question:

"We reiterate those words in this petition and for as long as the Constitution of Kenya has the provisions granting this Court the mandate to overturn a presidential election in appropriate circumstances, it will do so because the people of Kenya in the preamble to the Constitution adopted, enacted and gave unto themselves the Constitution for themselves and future generations."

Section 83 and the Disjunctive Interpretation

The pure theory of law is as Hans Kelsen argues in his book Pure Theory of Law — a theory that answers the question of the law as it is, not as it ought to be. It is a theory of interpretation as the Court applied it in interpreting Section 83 of the Elections Act, describing the law and eliminating from the law anything that is not strictly law. This theory further opines that the norm that confers upon an act the meaning of legality or illegality is itself created by an act, which in turn receives its legal character from yet another legal norm higher than it in the hierarchy of norms. That the validity of any statute or subsidiary legislation results in its compliance with the provisions of the Constitution.

Section 83 of the Act was the fulcrum of the petition as argued by Harrison Otieno. He further argues that the section is about two limbs: the qualitative and quantitative aspects of an election:

"The debate around section 83 has boiled down to whether the 'qualitative' versus the 'quantitative' approaches are disjunctive or conjunctive in answering the question whether an election is free and fair. The first part of section 83 has been termed qualitative; it is concerned with compliance with constitutional principles laid down in the Constitution and other electoral laws. The second limb is separated from the first by the conjunction 'or' and introduces the quantitative test; that non-compliance should affect the result of the election in order for the election to be voided."

The Court interpreted the section using the Pure Theory of Law — the law is as it is, not as it ought to be. They gave the section a literal interpretation. The Court pointed out that "clearly there are two limbs to section 83: compliance with the law on elections, and irregularities that may affect the result of the elections." The most important question was whether the provision in section 83 is "interpreted 'conjunctively' or 'disjunctively'." Since the conjunction used in section 83 is "or" and not "and," the test in section 83 is disjunctive, meaning that the petitioner need not prove both that the election was not held in conformity with the law and that such non-conformity affected the results, but only one of two. As the legal cliché applies: if the legislature intended otherwise, nothing would have been easier than to use the conjunction "and."

Constitutionalism and the Grundnorm

The majority separated from the law anything that is not the law as positivists believe. It held that an election is to be conducted substantially in accordance with the principles laid down in the Constitution, that voting also should accord with Article 86 of the Constitution. It further held that any election that never followed the procedural and substantive law is to be annulled as it is not an election irrespective of the outcome. The Court held thus:

"That expression in our view requires that, before vitiating it, the court should, looking at the conduct of the whole election, be satisfied that it substantially breached the principles in the Constitution, the Elections Act and other electoral law."

The Court emphasised the importance of the qualitative and quantitative aspects of the election complying with the Constitution. This was a regurgitation of Kelsen's view that norms are regulations setting forth how persons are to behave and positivism is thus a normative order regulating human conduct in a specific way. The Constitution and Section 83 of the Elections Act were to guide the conduct of the regulations, and since the elections never followed the regulation as dictated by the norms, they were invalidated by the Court.

It is therefore crystal clear that the majority of the justices who nullified the presidential election were influenced to a greater degree by the positivist school of thought. Among the many positivist principles that are emphatically clear in the judgement are: the application of the law as it is — the pure theory of law — and the enactment of the law by the people rather than natural law. The superiority of the Grundnorm — the Constitution — was central to the entire judgment.
